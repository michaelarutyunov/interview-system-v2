# Critique

Yes, there *is* a logic in the interviewer’s questions — but it’s also clearly showing some weaknesses that suggest the interviewer is either **inexperienced** or **over-relying on a rigid “laddering/ascend” script** rather than properly tracking the respondent’s story.

### What the interviewer is doing well (good logic)

**1) Starts with a strong critical incident opener.**
Turn 0 is a good “anchor question”: it invites a specific memory, not an opinion.

**2) Follows emotional meaning rather than product facts.**
From Turn 1 onward, the interviewer keeps probing *what it meant*, *how it felt*, *what changed*. That is consistent with critical incident + MEC-style laddering.

**3) The question flow is coherent in the emotional arc.**
It goes:

* discovery moment → surprise → reflection on habit/autopilot → repeat visits → novelty fading → deeper underlying need → validation summary

That’s a real narrative progression.

---

### Where the logic breaks / what feels off

**1) The focus node tracking is wrong / inconsistent.**
Several questions claim to be driven by the “traveling in Portland with roommate” situation, but the question content is not about that situation — it’s about the *realization* and *meaning*.

This is a signal the interviewer (or system) is not actually anchoring on the right node.

**2) Too many consecutive “ascend” questions = repetitive probing.**
Turns 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 are all basically:

* “what did that feel like?”
* “what did it mean?”
* “what did it tell you?”
* “what changed?”

That’s laddering, yes — but it becomes monotonous and “therapy-like”. A skilled interviewer would break the pattern with:

* sensory recall (“what did it taste like exactly?”)
* context (“who was around, what time of day?”)
* behavioral detail (“what did you order next time?”)
* contrast (“what would you have ordered otherwise?”)

**3) The respondent introduces a new element (barista remembering order) that doesn’t logically belong to the original incident.**
The original incident is: *first sip of roommate’s cold brew in Portland café.*

Then suddenly in Turn 5 the respondent says:

> “the barista remembered how I like it”

That implies repeat visits / relationship — which doesn’t match “first discovery” unless the respondent is blending multiple memories.

A strong interviewer would notice the timeline confusion and clarify:

* “Was this the same café in Portland?”
* “Was the barista recognition later, or part of that first trip?”

Instead, the interviewer just accepts it and builds the rest of the interview around it.

That’s a major qualitative interviewing flaw: **failure to resolve narrative inconsistencies**.

**4) The interview drifts away from cold brew into belonging / recognition.**
That’s not automatically wrong (it’s actually a good ladder), but it becomes *dominant* and leaves the product behind too early.

A more experienced interviewer would keep one foot grounded in the coffee experience:

* “What is it about cold brew specifically that fits that feeling?”
* “Would a cappuccino in that same café have produced the same emotional response?”
